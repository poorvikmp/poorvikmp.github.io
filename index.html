Preface

I'm Poorvik. I design and backtest systematic portfolio strategies, and I care more about whether a result holds up out of sample than whether it looks good.

Each chapter in this book is one study: the question, how I tested it, and what held up when I checked it on data I never tuned on. The failures are in there too, because they explain the results.

I work mostly in Python, and I'm looking for roles in quantitative research, portfolio analytics and investment strategy.

Regime-Based Tactical Allocation

Can inflation, yield-curve and VIX signals improve a 60/40 portfolio? I backtested 26 years of data, tuned only on 2000–2014, and stress-tested the result until I knew where the edge really came from.

0.72Test Sharpe vs 0.70
93%of random signals beaten
26 yrsbacktested, after costs

The finding. Version 2 edged out 60/40 in a test period I never tuned on, with the same drawdown. But most of that edge came from owning about 5% more stocks, not from reading the regime.

Chapter2

Cross-Asset Correlation Regime Decomposition Study

When does the stock-bond hedge fail? I measured how stocks, bonds and commodities move on 20 years of CPI releases and Fed decisions, used jobs-report days as a control, and tested whether you can trade it.

+0.55stock-bond corr. on CPI days
≈ 4×60/40's return on event days
658CPI, Fed and jobs days
  • Python
  • statsmodels
  • Event study
  • Macro

The finding. On CPI and Fed days in an inflation regime, stocks and bonds fall together. Stepping aside for those days doesn't pay, because they also carry the highest returns.

Chapter3

Anatomy of a Bubble: AI vs Dot-Com vs Housing

Is the AI boom a bubble? I measured it against the dot-com and housing bubbles with the same yardsticks, tested an early-warning signal on every US industry since 1926, and priced what protection costs a retail investor.

44%of the US market is tech (43% in 2000)
70%of the AI-era gain from earnings
69industry run-ups since 1926
  • Python
  • pandas
  • Market history
  • Risk

The finding. AI is an expensive, very concentrated boom with real earnings under it. Run-ups with 3–4 red flags crashed 80% of the time after 1990; chips show 2 of 4 today.

How I work

I'm drawn to the question underneath every backtest: is this result real, or did I just find a pattern in noise? Three rules keep me honest.

  1. Tune on one period, judge on another.The test data stays untouched until the end.
  2. Beat the simple alternative.A strategy has to beat what you could do with no signals at all.
  3. Report what didn't work.The failures explain the result as much as the wins.
